# Story and attention

Rules learned from real ad reviews and a live campaign (a cohort course, Sep 2026). Each one cost a re-render, a
paid generation or a review round. Where a rule is a judgement rather than a measurement, it says so.

## The brief comes first: buyer, decision, mechanism

- An ad that makes the viewer decode a clever comparison before giving them a reason to care loses them. In the
  reviewed newsjack videos the product name appeared at 14-15 s of 16-18 s: the viewer could watch almost all of it
  without learning why it mattered to them (external creative review). Name what is sold and the buyer's stake by the
  middle of the ad; the end card confirms and gives dates.
- Write the buyer's decision and the cost of getting it wrong before any headline. A line that only names the
  product's vocabulary ("an archetype", "the five steps") demonstrates nothing; show the decision the buyer will be
  able to make.
- Draft three different approaches before choosing (news-led, buyer-problem, product demonstration). The first idea
  is rarely the one to fund, and a newsjack is only one of them.

## Frame 0 and the first seconds

- Frame 0 must explain the setup by itself, with no labels. "A cohort 1 participant" meant nothing; "The manager is on
  holiday. A client calls." worked.
- Frame 0 must already show the hook: animations that start at delay 0 leave the autoplay thumbnail empty. Start
  first-scene elements at a negative delay (`useIn`, `Say first`).
- Put an object on frame 0 when you can (the product, a mock of it, the illustration of the news). Two lines of text
  over an empty frame read as unfinished (an adopter's first render; fixed by moving the product mock to frame 0).

## Reading

- Viewers skip small kicker labels. Anything that carries meaning goes in a full-size line; a pivot line ("then he
  remembered his model") gets its own beat. Flagged twice in one review.
- A 1080 px frame shows at about a third of its size on a phone. Anything the viewer must read is at least 44 px
  (`MIN_TEXT`); URL and author lines at least 32 px. Diagram labels count: a handwritten 50 px label on a surface
  scaled to 0.84 reads at about 14 px on a 360 px-wide phone, too small.
- A caption must not cover what it describes: a full-width dark band hid the water under the word "Ice". Compact
  panels placed away from the action fixed it.
- The punch must bridge to the offer: after the moral line, the end card starts with a large lead ("How to get out of
  this loop, we cover in the course"), then the product and the concrete offer.
- If a line needs explaining, rewrite it. Invented numbers read as data: remove them, or mark them on screen as an
  example.

## Motion and sound

- Unrelated motion steals attention from the line next to it (a ringing phone versus the caption). Stop it while the
  viewer reads. Motion that points at the line being read (a circle drawn around the word, an underline) helps.
- Environmental generated effects (frost, steam, light, water) come out well; hand-object manipulation (turning a
  knob, pulling a lever) often does not. Prefer stories that rely on the first.
- Make every ad work with the sound off: most placements start muted. Sound is not forbidden; add it as a deliberate
  variant and test it, rather than assuming either way.

## Truth in the story

- Every on-screen claim traces to a source line. A transcript can contradict itself; the author's correction wins.
  For a personal story, read the key fact back to the author before building (an ad shipped the opposite of what
  happened: "people not from the US" instead of "people from the US").
- Author's literal assets beat abstraction: the product's own game map with animated fleets beat an optimizer
  statistics angle; a photographed sheet beats vector art.
